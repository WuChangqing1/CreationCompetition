Abstract
Depression is an increasingly prevalent mental health issue world-
wide, especially among the elderly, where effective early detection
is crucial for timely intervention. While recent multimodal ap-
proaches demonstrate promise in leveraging visual and auditory
cues for automatic depression recognition, existing methods often
fail to extract fine-grained, depression-specific patterns from pre-
extracted features and underutilize cross-modal interactions. To
address these challenges, we propose DepFormer, a unified frame-
work that incorporates a Bimodal Collaborative Transformer (BCT)
for cross-modal representation learning and a personalized fusion
module to enhance individual-specific modeling. The architecture
comprises: (1) unimodal feature extraction, (2) bimodal collabora-
tive representation learning via the BCT, (3) personalized feature
fusion, and (4) final depression classification. Critically, the BCT
employs symmetric bidirectional branches comprising an Audio-
to-Video Transformer and a Video-to-Audio Transformer to enable
mutual enhancement and complementary learning across modal-
ities. Extensive experiments validate DepFormer’s effectiveness,
securing first place in the MPDD Challenge 2025 (Elderly Track),
underscoring its strong practical potential.

1 Introduction
Depression is a common and disabling mental disorder marked
by persistent sadness, loss of interest, and cognitive decline. Ac-
cording to the World Health Organization, over 280 million people
worldwide suffer from depression, with incidence rates continuing
to rise, particularly among the elderly1. If not properly treated,
depression can lead to severe outcomes such as social withdrawal,
reduced quality of life, and even suicide. These risks underscore the
need for early and reliable detection. Given that traditional clinical
interviews for depression are costly and unscalable, particularly in
the face of a growing patient population, the development of an
automated detection system has become an urgent need [1].
Automated depression detection based on multimodal data (e.g.,
video, audio, and text) has shown great promise. Existing research
mainly focused on two key aspects: extracting depression-relevant
features from each modality and effectively fusing these heteroge-
neous features into a unified representation. Early works employed
CNNs and RNNs to capture spatial and temporal patterns [ 2–8],
while recent studies leverage Transformer-based architectures and
state-space models like Mamba for improved long-range temporal
modeling [ 9–15]. Regarding fusion, methods vary from decision-
level aggregation of unimodal predictions [ 16–18] to feature-level
concatenation or projection before classification [ 16–18]. More
recent approaches explore model-level fusion with cross-modal
attention mechanisms to enable richer interaction [10, 15, 19, 20].
While these methods have achieved promising results, they still
face two key limitations: (1) insufficient extraction of depression-
relevant patterns from pre-extracted features (e.g., MFCCs, Open-
Face), which often suffer from semantic loss and limited expressive-
ness due to the unavailability of raw data under privacy constraints;
and (2) sub-optimal multimodal fusion strategies that fail to fully

2 Related Work
2.1 Multimodal Depression Detection
Automatic depression detection has attracted increasing attention
in recent years, especially with the availability of multimodal be-
havioral data such as audio and video. However, unlike many other
multimodal tasks, depression detection often involves sensitive
information, making the collection and release of raw audio and
video data difficult due to privacy concerns. As a result, datasetssuch as DAIC-WOZ [ 21], D-Vlog [ 10] and MPDD [ 9] provide only
pre-extracted features instead of raw video and audio, which brings
new challenges to depression detection.
Early studies on multimodal depression detection relied on hand-
crafted features. Visual cues were captured using local descriptors
like LBP and LBP-TOP [ 22,23], while MFCCs [ 24] and LIWC [ 25]
were commonly used for audio and text, respectively. Although
interpretable and easy to implement, these methods depend heavily
on manually designed features, limiting their capacity to model
complex patterns and generalize to real-world scenarios.
Recent advances in deep learning enable richer representation
learning from multimodal features. To model temporal visual dy-
namics, CNN-based methods have been widely explored, typically
using 3D CNNs or 2D CNNs combined with temporal modules such
as attentive convolutions [ 2–5]. However, these methods are limited
in capturing long-range dependencies. To address this, RNN-based
models, particularly those using LSTM units, have been introduced
to better capture temporal progression in visual and acoustic cues.
These methods [ 6–8] leverage sequential modeling to capture dy-
namic changes in mood-related cues. While such models enhance
sequential modeling, their generalization to real-world depression
analysis remains limited, especially under diverse subject condi-
tions and varying recording environments [26].
More recently, Transformer-based architectures [ 9–14] and Mamba-
based models [ 15] have been applied to depression detection, offer-
ing enhanced long-range dependency modeling through attention
or state-space mechanisms. For example, Yoon et al. [ 10] proposed
a Transformer-based multimodal model with cross-attention to
effectively capture relationships across acoustic and visual features,
while Ye et al. [ 15] introduced DepMamba, leveraging Mamba for
hierarchical contextual modeling and progressive fusion. Building
on these developments, we introduce a unified framework incorpo-
rating a Bimodal Collaborative Transformer for bimodal represen-
tation learning and personalized depression severity classification.
2.2 Multimodal Fusion Methods
In recent years, multimodal fusion techniques, which integrate cues
from heterogeneous sources such as audio and video, have become
important for improving depression detection. These multimodal

fusion approaches can typically be categorized into feature-level,
decision-level, and model-level fusion strategies.
Feature-level fusion involves concatenating features from vari-
ous modalities to learn a unified representation before classifica-
tion [ 16–18]. For instance, Fu et al. [ 16] fused facial action units and
speech features by extracting complementary emotional features
by integrating them via a graph-based temporal attention model to
enhance depression detection. However, feature-level fusion may
suffer from feature incompatibility and high dimensionality when
combining heterogeneous modalities.
Alternatively, decision-level fusion ensembles the predictions
from models trained on single modalities to reach a collective classi-
fication [ 27–30]. Zhang et al. [ 28] proposed a decision-level method
using a multi-agent system. While straightforward, decision-level
fusion lacks deep interaction between modalities, potentially limit-
ing its ability to capture subtle cross-modal correlations.
Model-level fusion incorporates cross-modal interactions within
the model architecture, allowing more integrated learning [ 10,15,
19,20]. Tao et al. [ 20] proposed DepMSTAT, a model-level fusion
framework that jointly captures spatio-temporal acoustic and vi-
sual features with a unified transformer for depression detection.
In contrast, our work explores a different direction by leverag-
ing a bimodal collaborative transformer to dynamically enhance
modality-specific representations through mutual refinement, fur-
ther incorporating personalized features for robust classification.
3 Methodology
In this section, we present our proposed DepFormer, a unified multi-
modal depression detection framework, which integrates acoustic,
video, and personalized features to achieve effective depression
detection. The core idea of our model is to first extract modality-
specific temporal representations, then enhance them through a
Bimodal Collaborative Transformer, and finally incorporate person-
alized information to form a unified representation for final classifi-
cation. The entire architecture consists of four key stages: Unimodal
Feature Extraction, Bimodal Collaborative Representation Learn-
ing, Personalized Feature Fusion, and Depression Classification. An
overview of the model is illustrated in Fig. 1.
3.1 Unimodal Feature Extraction
In the first stage of our model, we encode acoustic and video tem-
poral sequences independently. Given the inherently sequential
nature of both audio and video data, we adopt Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) networks to capture temporal dependencies within
each modality. This allows the model to learn modality-specific
representations that preserve important temporal dynamics.

3.3 Personalized Feature Fusion
As the MPDD dataset provides static personalized features P∈R𝑑𝑝,
which capture participant-specific information. To integrate these
features with the dynamic bimodal representations, we first project
the original personalized features P, which have dimension 𝑑𝑝, into
the model’s hidden feature space R𝑑𝑚𝑜𝑑𝑒𝑙 via a linear layer:
P′=Linear(P),P′∈R𝑑model. (10)The personalized embedding P′is then concatenated with the
bimodal collaborative feature Fbimodal to form the final feature rep-

Ffinal=Concat(Fbimodal,P′). (11)
This fusion strategy allows the model to leverage both the static
participant-specific attributes and the dynamic audio-visual fea-
tures for improved prediction.

To improve robustness and mitigate class imbalance, we adopt a
dual-head classification strategy. The final fused representation
Ffinalis fed into two parallel classifiers: one trained with cross-
entropy loss and the other with focal loss. Formally, the two sets of
logits are given by:
zCE=Classifier CE(Ffinal),zFL=Classifier FL(Ffinal), (12)
where zCEandzFLare the logits predicted by the two classifiers.
The total training loss is defined as:

whereLCEandLFocal denote the cross-entropy and focal loss func-

contribution of the focal loss.
Only the Classifier CEis used during inference, while both heads

4 Experimentation
4.1 Dataset and Evaluation Metrics
The MPDD-Elderly dataset is a multimodal dataset focused on de-
pression detection in elderly individuals. It includes audio-visual
recordings and personalized information to better capture depression-
related cues while considering individual differences. Each sample
is associated with all three classification schemes: binary (normal vs.
depressed), ternary (normal, mild, severe), and quinary (five-level
PHQ-9 severity).
For evaluation, we adopt two standard classification metrics:
Accuracy (Acc) and F1 Score (F1). Both weighted and unweighted
versions are calculated to evaluate performance on the MPDD-
Elderly dataset.
4.2 Implementation Details
We evaluate our model on three classification tasks—binary, ternary,
and quinary—under two split window settings (1-second and 5-
second clips). The model is implemented in PyTorch and trained
using either the Adam or AdamW optimizer, with hyperparameters
summarized in Table 2.
Our model is implemented using PyTorch. All experiments are
conducted on a machine equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
Guided by empirical results, we set the hidden dimension 𝑑model to
256, the number of Transformer layers 𝑁to 1, and the number of
attention heads ℎto 2 to balance efficiency and performance.. For
input features, we use DenseNet-based visual features for the video

We compare our method with the official baseline and a recent
state-of-the-art model, DepMamba [ 15], which adopts a progressive
audio-visual fusion strategy for multimodal depression detection.

4.4 Performance Fluctuation Analysis
Given the inherent variability among participants in the MPDD-
Elderly dataset (e.g., health conditions, personal traits), we observe
noticeable performance fluctuations across different training runs.
To provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we evaluate all mod-
els on the MPDD-Elderly test set by repeating each experiment 10
times, reporting the mean and standard deviation of Accuracy and
F1, and conducting paired t-tests to assess significance.
As shown in Table 3, compared to DepMamba, our method gen-
erally achieves higher average accuracy and F1 scores across all
tasks. For example, in the 5s quinary classification task, our model
reaches an average accuracy of 68.09% with a standard deviation of
8.26, while DepMamba achieves 55.20% with a larger fluctuation
(std = 17.91). A similar pattern is observed in the F1 score (65.95 ±
7.97 vs. 50.04 ±19.00). These improvements are not only substan-
tial but also statistically significant in most tasks and metrics, as
indicated by paired t-tests.

5 Conclusion
This paper presents a unified framework for multimodal depression
detection, aiming to enhance the learning of depression-relevant
features from pre-extracted multimodal inputs and to improve
cross-modal fusion. To this end, we propose a Bimodal Collabora-
tive Transformer that facilitates bidirectional interaction between
modalities, along with a personalized fusion strategy to incorporate
individual-specific information. Experimental results on the MPDD
benchmark demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art
performance and ranks first in the MPDD-Elderly track, confirm-
ing its effectiveness and practical value for privacy-constrained,
real-world applications.
